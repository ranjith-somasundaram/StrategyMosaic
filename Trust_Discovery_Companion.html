Trust-First Discovery

From surface-level answers
to commercial truth

Trust creates disclosure. Disclosure creates value proof. Value proof protects price. Everything in this guide serves that single chain.

The foundation

Why trust comes before everything

Value Engineering and Cost Deflection are both zero-value exercises without trust. You cannot build a credible business case from numbers someone gave you reluctantly.

Value Engineering
The business case needs their data
Value IQ is only credible if the inputs are real. Fabricated baselines get challenged. Customer-owned numbers get signed off. To get real numbers, you need real trust. The data you need: baseline spend, query volumes, compute costs, time lost to manual processes, revenue leakage, risk exposure, and who owns the problem.
Cost Deflection
Savings need to be believed, not just calculated
Cost deflection means avoiding future cost growth, reducing unnecessary spend, or preventing inefficient consumption — such as redundant queries hitting Snowflake or Databricks when MOSAIC could serve the result. The customer has to believe the number. Belief requires trust in you, not just trust in the maths.
What "everything is fine" actually signals
It means: I don't know you well enough to tell you it isn't. Cloud spend is politically sensitive. Governance failures are embarrassing. Admitting a project is at risk requires trusting you won't use it against them. The default position of any prospect you've just met is self-protection. Your job in the first 15 minutes is to lower that protection — not with charm, but with competence and empathy.
The sequence that works
1
Demonstrate you already understand their world
Before asking a single question. Name their industry's pain. Reference a peer. Show you've done the homework. This alone creates more trust than 20 minutes of small talk.
2
Ask the flip question
The one question that moves them from performance mode to honest mode. Not a pitch. Not a product question. A mirror that makes them feel seen before they've said anything real.
3
Mirror and label what they say
Make them feel heard so deeply they keep going. Resist every urge to solve. You are a doctor who hasn't finished the examination — prescribing now would be premature.
4
Extend the diagnostic phase
Until you've heard more than one symptom. The first problem they name is rarely the one that drives the decision. Keep the channel open.
5
Confirm the problem in their words before building value
Reflect back what you heard — using their language, not yours. This step separates trust-based discovery from a pitch with questions in it. Only then does Value Engineering begin.
Before the flip question

Read the room

The same question lands completely differently depending on who you're talking to. Take 90 seconds to assess the persona before you go in — then direct your opening to the person closest to the operational pain.

When multiple personas are in the room
Address the most senior person first as a courtesy — but listen for the person closest to the operational pain, then invite their perspective respectfully. That person is carrying the real weight. The senior person will watch how they respond before deciding whether to engage themselves.
The craft moment

The flip question

There is no single magic question. But there is a pattern — and once you see the structure, you can build one on the spot for any room you walk into.

"It removes the risk of disclosure by making the problem belong to everyone first."
Before they can admit a problem, they need to know it's safe to admit it. The flip question normalises the pain as an industry pattern — not their failure. They're not confessing anything. They're agreeing with an observation. That is a completely different psychological act.
The anatomy
01
Normalise first
"Most [industry] teams we talk to…"
They are not alone. Whatever they're about to admit is common. The perceived risk drops immediately. This one phrase does more work than five minutes of rapport-building.
02
Name something specific and slightly uncomfortable
"…are dealing with [precise pain, not a generic category]."
Vague pains get vague nods. Specific pains get a flicker of recognition. If you can name the symptom — not just the category — you've demonstrated you understand their world.
03
Open door — not a trap
"…does any of that resonate at all?"
"At all" is doing serious work here. It makes yes easy and no awkward. They can approach from the side — they don't have to walk straight through the door. This is the difference between a question and an interrogation.
The customer story — most powerful variant
A true, anonymised story about another customer is a mirror. They can see themselves in it without having to say so. The story must be accurate — never invent facts. Anonymise the client where needed, but the commercial situation must be real.
Situation
"A bank in this region, similar size to yours…"
The friction
"…Snowflake bill growing 40% QoQ, nobody could identify which workload was driving it…"
The cost
"The CFO was asking questions the data team couldn't answer."
The mirror
"Sound familiar at all?" — then stop. Give them space to respond.
After the flip question — give space
The question lands and creates a gap. The gap is the mechanism. Do not fill it immediately. Hold eye contact. What comes into that space — a laugh, a pause, a glance between colleagues, a single word — is the real beginning of the conversation. Use silence to give space, not to create pressure.

Negotiation psychology

Mirror & label

People don't open up when they feel questioned. They open up when they feel understood. Use these techniques to understand — not to manipulate. The goal is clarity, not control.

Mirroring — repeat their last 2–3 words
They say: "We've been trying to get visibility into our cloud costs for about a year now."
"About a year?"
Two words and a slight upward inflection. They now feel compelled to explain the year — the delays, the politics, the failed attempts. Often encourages elaboration when delivered with genuine curiosity.
They say: "The business keeps asking for numbers we can't always stand behind."
"Numbers you can't stand behind."
Not a question — a statement. Slightly slower delivery. They'll tell you which numbers and exactly why they can't defend them.
They say: "It's complicated because of how our data is structured."
"How it's structured…"
Leaves the sentence open. This gives them a natural opening to explain further — without pressure or direction from you.
Labelling — name what's underneath
They describe a painful situation in careful, controlled language.
"It sounds like this has been frustrating for a while."
Naming the emotion they're suppressing. If accurate, they confirm and go deeper. If slightly off, they correct you — either way, they keep talking.
They mention the board keeps asking about cloud costs.
"It sounds like there's pressure from above that hasn't been easy to respond to."
Always start labels with "It sounds like…" or "It seems like…" — never "I can see that you feel…" which sounds clinical.
They describe something technical in a way that seems defensive.
"It seems like the current setup is holding some things back."
Names the constraint without assigning blame. Almost always accurate. They'll confirm and describe the constraint in their own terms.
Follow with a calibrated question
After mirroring and labelling, follow with a "what" or "how" question — never "why" (sounds accusatory) and never yes/no (closes the door).
"What does that look like for your team on a typical month?"
"How long has that been the case?"
"What happens when the business challenges the number?"
"How does that affect the relationship with the business?"
"What have you tried to resolve it?"
"What happens if this doesn't get resolved in the next 12 months?"
The full journey

Disclosure to commitment

Most people stop at the first disclosure and then pitch. That is the mistake. The distance between "yes we have that problem" and "we need to do something about it" is where the real work happens. Click each stage.

Disclosure → Diagnosis
They've named one problem. You usually need more than one symptom before diagnosing the real business issue. Keep the channel open: "Say more about that" / "Who else feels that?" / "What have you tried?" Stay in diagnostic mode until the picture is complete.
Diagnosis → Urgency
You've heard the full picture. Create urgency — but not by asserting it. Ask: "What happens if this doesn't get resolved in the next 12 months?" or "If you could put a number on the cost of this today — what would you do with it?" They articulate the urgency themselves. That is more powerful than any claim you could make.
Urgency → Mutual Commitment
The goal is for them to say some version of: "We really need to sort this out." Once they do — that is your moment. Not to pitch. To confirm and offer the next step: "Here is what I heard: [their words]. Here is what that may be costing. Here is what we'd need to validate together. Does that feel like the right next conversation?"
The Value Engineering handoff
The transition into business case work should feel like a natural next step they suggested — not a presentation you prepared. When they're at Urgency: "What we normally do at this point is run a structured exercise to put a real number on what this costs today, and what deflecting it would save. Would that be useful?"
Protect the trust you've built

What not to say

Every one of these phrases signals that the pitch is coming. The moment they hear it, trust evaporates. Know them. Recognise them in yourself. Avoid them.

"We have a solution for that."
Signals you stopped listening and started selling. They feel like bait — like the question was a trap to reach this line. The disclosure door closes and rarely reopens in the same meeting.
Instead: "That makes sense. Say more about how that plays out." Stay in diagnostic mode. There is no rush.
"Other customers use us for exactly this."
Positions you as a vendor, not a thinking partner. They stop feeling unique. Customer stories work powerfully before disclosure — as the flip question vehicle. After disclosure, they read as a pitch.
Instead: Use the customer story before they share anything, to create the safe space. Not after, to validate your product.
"Let me show you how MOSAIC works."
Premature product introduction. They haven't told you what they need yet. Any demo at this point is a gamble — you may be showing them precisely the wrong thing and losing the diagnostic moment.
Instead: "Before I show you anything — I want to make sure what I'm showing is relevant to your actual situation. Can I ask a few more questions first?"
"That's a great question."
When used as a filler reflex it signals performance, not listening. The trust gap widens. If you say it, mean it — and follow it with a real response or a mirror, not a pivot to your talking points.
Instead: Mirror what they said. A slow nod. A pause. These signal genuine engagement. Empty affirmations signal a script.
"We can reduce your costs by X%."
A number without their data behind it is a claim, not a fact. They'll either dismiss it or demand proof you can't yet provide. Either way, credibility drops precisely when you need it most.
Instead: "Based on what you've described — would it be worth putting a real number on this together?" Let the number come from a joint exercise they trust.
"So what's your timeline and budget?"
Too early. This signals you're qualifying them for your pipeline, not helping them solve their problem. The trust you've spent 20 minutes building evaporates in one sentence. Timeline and budget will emerge naturally when they feel the urgency themselves — you rarely need to ask directly.
Instead: Ask "What happens if this doesn't get resolved in the next 12 months?" Urgency and timeline surface from that question without making them feel like a prospect being processed.
Close the loop

Trust readiness check

How do you know if trust has actually been earned — or if you just had a polite conversation? Use this checklist after every discovery meeting to assess whether you've reached real disclosure.

0 of 8 signals — keep building
If you scored 6–8
Real disclosure has happened. You have the material to build a credible Value Engineering exercise. Move to confirming what you heard in their words — then propose the next step.
If you scored 3–5
Partial disclosure. You have enough to re-engage but not enough to build on. Go back with a more specific flip question or a customer story targeted at the pain you sensed but didn't fully surface.
If you scored 0–2
The conversation stayed surface-level. Don't move to Value Engineering — you don't have the inputs yet. Reset the trust conversation before the next meeting. Consider whether you had the right persona in the room.
