Why trust comes before everything
The principle: The purpose of this framework is not to manipulate the buyer. It is to create enough trust for the buyer to tell the truth — so that the value you build together is real, defensible, and signed off.
Value Engineering
Value IQ is only credible if the inputs come from the customer. To get real numbers — baseline spend, compute costs, time lost, risk exposure — you need real trust first.
Cost Deflection
Cost deflection means avoiding future spend growth or eliminating redundant consumption. The customer has to believe the number. Belief requires trust in you, not just the maths.
1
Demonstrate you understand their world
Before asking anything. Name their industry's pain. Reference a peer. Show you've done the homework.
2
Ask the flip question
The one question that moves them from performance mode to honest mode. Not a pitch — a mirror.
3
Mirror and label what they say
Make them feel heard deeply enough that they keep going. Resist every urge to solve.
4
Extend the diagnostic phase
Until you've heard more than one problem. The first thing they name is rarely the one that drives the decision.
5
Confirm in their words before building value
Reflect back what you heard using their language. Only then does Value Engineering begin.
Read the room
Multiple personas present
Address the most senior person first — but listen for the person closest to the operational pain, then invite their perspective. That person is carrying the real weight.
The flip question
01
Normalise first
"Most [industry] teams we talk to…"
They are not alone. Whatever they're about to admit is common. The perceived risk drops immediately.
02
Name something specific and uncomfortable
"…are dealing with [precise pain, not a generic category]."
Vague pains get vague nods. Specific pains get a flicker of recognition. Naming the symptom proves you understand their world.
03
Open door — not a trap
"…does any of that resonate at all?"
"At all" makes yes easy and no awkward. They can approach from the side — not walk straight through the door.
The customer story variant
Situation
"A bank in this region, similar size to yours…"
The friction
"…Snowflake bill growing 40% QoQ, nobody knew which workload was driving it…"
The cost
"The CFO was asking questions the data team couldn't answer."
The mirror
"Sound familiar at all?" — then stop. Give them space.
After the flip question
Do not fill the silence. Hold eye contact. What comes into that space — a laugh, a pause, a glance between colleagues — is the real beginning of the conversation. Use silence to give space, not create pressure.
On customer stories
The story must be true and anonymised where needed. Never invent facts. The commercial situation must be real — only the client name changes.
Question bank
Mirror & label
Mirroring — repeat their last 2–3 words
They say: "We've been trying to get visibility into our cloud costs for about a year now."
"About a year?"
Two words and an upward inflection. They now feel compelled to explain the year — the delays, the politics, the failed attempts.
They say: "It's complicated because of how our data is structured."
"How it's structured…"
Leaves the sentence open. This gives them a natural opening to explain further without pressure.
Labelling — name what's underneath
They describe a painful situation in careful, controlled language.
"It sounds like this has been frustrating for a while."
Name the emotion they're suppressing. If accurate, they go deeper. If slightly off, they correct you — either way, they keep talking.
They describe something technical in a way that seems defensive.
"It seems like the current setup is holding some things back."
Names the constraint without assigning blame. Almost always accurate. Always start labels with "It sounds like…" or "It seems like…"
Follow with a calibrated question
Always "what" or "how" — never "why" (accusatory) and never yes/no (closes the door).
"What does that look like for your team on a typical month?"
"How long has that been the case?"
"What have you tried to resolve it?"
The Value Engineering handoff
When they say "we really need to sort this out" — that is your moment. Not to pitch. To confirm: "Here is what I heard. Here is what that may be costing. Here is what we'd need to validate together. Would that be a useful next conversation?"
What not to say
"We have a solution for that."
Signals you stopped listening and started selling. The disclosure door closes and rarely reopens in the same meeting.
Instead: "That makes sense. Say more about how that plays out."
"Other customers use us for exactly this."
Positions you as a vendor. Customer stories work before disclosure — as the flip question vehicle. After disclosure, they read as a pitch.
Instead: Use the story before they share anything, to create the safe space.
"Let me show you how MOSAIC works."
Premature. They haven't told you what they need. Any demo now is a gamble — you may be showing the wrong thing entirely.
Instead: "Before I show anything — can I ask a few more questions to make sure it's relevant?"
"We can reduce your costs by X%."
A number without their data is a claim, not a fact. They'll dismiss it or demand proof you can't yet provide.
Instead: "Would it be worth putting a real number on this together?"
"So what's your timeline and budget?"
Too early. Signals you're qualifying them for your pipeline. Timeline and budget emerge naturally when they feel the urgency themselves.
Instead: "What happens if this doesn't get resolved in the next 12 months?"
"Let me send you a proposal."
Signals the discovery conversation is over before it's complete. A proposal sent too early is built on assumptions — and assumptions get challenged at procurement. It also hands over control of the narrative to a document they'll read without you.
Instead: "Before I put anything together — can we agree on what success looks like? That way what I send is actually useful."
"We're the market leader in this space."
Instantly activates vendor mode. Every claim of leadership invites the question "according to who?" and shifts the conversation from their problem to your positioning. It also makes you sound like everyone else who walks into that room.
Instead: Let the customer story do the credibility work. A specific, relevant example outperforms any market ranking.
"Let me connect you with our account team."
Tells them the real conversation hasn't started yet — and that you were just a qualifier. It breaks whatever trust the discovery conversation built and signals that what comes next will feel more like a sales process.
Instead: Stay in the conversation. If a handoff is needed, make it warm, contextual, and with you present — not a cold introduction.
